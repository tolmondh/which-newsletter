1. Decide if migration is worth it

Buttondown is a lean, Markdown-friendly newsletter tool that many indie writers and developers like for its simplicity and API. beehiiv is built for growing and monetizing a newsletter: a hosted publication site and archive, referral and recommendation growth, and revenue through ads, Boosts, sponsorships, and paid subscriptions.

Moving usually makes sense when:

  • You want growth tools (referrals, recommendations) instead of building them yourself.
  • You want ad, Boost, or sponsorship revenue to become a real line item.
  • You’d rather use a visual editor and templates than hand-write Markdown and HTML each issue.

Stay put if you love writing in Markdown, depend on Buttondown’s API or webhooks in custom tooling, or your list is small and happy and a move would cost time without unlocking anything you’ll use. Still deciding? Read beehiiv vs Buttondown first.

2. Honest caveats

Expect some friction. Don’t assume a clean lift-and-shift.

  • Your writing workflow changes. If you draft in Markdown, test how your formatting, code blocks, and footnotes look in beehiiv’s editor before committing. Some styling may need manual cleanup.
  • Archive URLs change. Posts on your Buttondown archive (or a custom domain pointed at it) will live at new beehiiv URLs. Plan redirects or at least link the old archive to the new one.
  • Tags and metadata need mapping. Buttondown tags and custom metadata won’t become beehiiv tags or custom fields automatically. Carry what you need as CSV columns.
  • API scripts and webhooks stop working. Anything that calls Buttondown’s API (signup forms, Zapier/Make hooks, static-site build steps) must be rewired to beehiiv or retired.
  • Paid subscriptions are the risky part. Billing moves need care so nobody loses access or gets charged twice (see section 8).
  • Suppressions still apply. Unsubscribed and bounced addresses must stay suppressed. Never re-permission people who opted out.

3. Inventory your Buttondown account

  1. List what’s actually in use Subscriber tags, custom metadata fields, any automations or scheduled emails, embedded forms, your archive domain, API integrations, and paid tiers.
  2. Mark each item keep, rebuild, or drop Old tags and test integrations pile up. Migration is a good time to retire them.
  3. Note everything tied to payments Paid subscribers, coupons, comp subscriptions, and the Stripe account behind them.
  4. Create the beehiiv publication Set branding, sender name, reply-to address, and a welcome email before importing anyone.

4. Export from Buttondown

Typical path (verify in Buttondown’s current subscribers and settings screens, and its export docs):

  • Export active subscribers as CSV with email, signup date, tags, and any metadata you’ll keep.
  • Export unsubscribed and bounced subscribers separately so they’re never imported as active.
  • Export or save your archive (past emails) so you have a backup and can republish selected posts.
  • Note which subscribers are paid, comped, or on trial; you’ll need that list for the billing step.

Clean the CSV before import: normalize email casing, remove duplicates and obvious junk, and keep one clear status column. Store the raw exports offline as a backup.

5. Map tags, metadata, and status

Build a simple mapping table before you import. For example:

  • Buttondown tag → beehiiv tag or custom field. Keep only tags you’ll actually target.
  • Metadata fields → beehiiv custom fields. Create the fields in beehiiv first so the importer can map them.
  • Subscriber type → separate files. Keep free, paid, and comped readers in separate CSVs so the paid ones can follow the billing plan.
  • Source tag. Add a tag like “migrated-buttondown” so you can tell migrated readers from new signups later.

6. Import into beehiiv

  1. Use beehiiv’s subscriber import Upload the cleaned free-subscriber CSV, map columns to fields, and apply your tags. Check beehiiv’s current docs for import limits or review steps on your plan.
  2. Decide on the welcome email Usually a single “we’ve moved” note is better than putting existing readers through a new-subscriber sequence.
  3. Respect consent rules Import only people who opted in, and follow beehiiv’s import policy and the email laws where you and your readers live.
  4. Spot-check counts Compare the imported total and a few tagged records against your Buttondown export before sending anything.

7. Archive, Markdown, and old links

  • Pick what to republish. You don’t have to bring every past issue. Your best evergreen posts are worth moving; check beehiiv’s docs for any post import options before copying by hand.
  • Check formatting. Paste a few Markdown-heavy issues into beehiiv and fix headings, code, images, and footnotes before deciding on a bulk approach.
  • Keep old links alive. If your archive sits on a custom domain, point it at beehiiv once the publication is ready, and redirect or link old post URLs where your setup allows. At minimum, leave a note on the old archive pointing to the new one.
  • Update signup points. Replace Buttondown forms and links on your website, social bios, and any static-site templates with beehiiv forms or your publication link.
  • Turn on growth features later. Once the list is stable, consider beehiiv’s referral program and recommendations if they fit your plan. See how to monetize a newsletter on beehiiv.

8. Paid subscribers and Stripe

If readers pay you through Buttondown (via Stripe), slow down here:

  • Check both vendors’ current docs on whether your existing Stripe account and subscriptions can connect to beehiiv, and what does or doesn’t carry over.
  • Don’t promise paying readers uninterrupted access until you’ve confirmed the billing path and tested it with a real subscription.
  • Avoid double-billing: make sure nobody gets charged by both systems after cutover, and decide how to handle comps and trials.
  • Tell paid readers in plain language what changes for them, if anything, before you switch.

9. Domain, DNS, and sender reputation

  • Add the SPF/DKIM records beehiiv asks for (and a DMARC record if you don’t have one) at your DNS host.
  • If Buttondown authenticated the same sending domain, leave its records in place until cutover is finished, then remove the ones you no longer need. A domain should publish a single SPF record.
  • Keep the same sender name and a familiar from-address so readers recognize you in the inbox.
  • For larger lists, consider sending the first beehiiv issues to your most engaged readers first, and check beehiiv’s guidance on warming up.

10. Cutover and communication

  1. Announce from Buttondown first (optional) A short heads-up from your current sender helps readers recognize the new emails.
  2. Pick a cutover date and stop dual-sending After that date, Buttondown is for archive and suppression reference only.
  3. Send the first beehiiv issue Include a brief “you’re in the right place” note, an easy unsubscribe, and a reply address for questions.
  4. Watch the first few sends Monitor bounces, complaints, and replies. Fix authentication issues right away and suppress hard bounces.
  5. Close Buttondown last Keep the account (or full exports) until paid billing, archive links, and integrations are confirmed, then downgrade or close it.

Final checklist

  • Active, unsubscribed, bounced, and paid exports saved offline
  • Archive backed up; evergreen posts chosen for republishing
  • Tag/metadata mapping table written down
  • Import complete, tagged “migrated-buttondown”, counts spot-checked
  • Sending domain authenticated on beehiiv; single SPF record
  • Archive domain and old links pointed or redirected
  • API scripts, webhooks, and forms rewired or retired
  • Paid subscriber billing path confirmed (if applicable)
  • Readers notified; Buttondown dual-send stopped
  • First beehiiv issue tested and sent

If growth and monetization are the goal (referrals, recommendations, ad and sponsor revenue, and a publication site), beehiiv is the path this guide assumes. If you mainly want a minimal, Markdown-first tool with a simple API, Buttondown may still be the better fit.
