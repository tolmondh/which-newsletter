1. Decide if a move fits

On WordPress, your newsletter is usually a plugin bolted onto a website: it emails new posts or campaigns to subscribers, and sending may run through the plugin’s own service, your host, or a separate email provider. beehiiv is built around the newsletter itself, with its own publication site, sending, analytics, and growth and monetization tools such as referrals, recommendations, ads, and paid subscriptions.

A move usually makes sense when:

  • The newsletter has become the main thing, and the website mostly exists to collect signups.
  • You’re tired of maintaining plugins, sending limits, or deliverability fixes on your host.
  • You want built-in growth tools or newsletter revenue options rather than assembling them from several plugins.
  • You want clearer email analytics than your current plugin gives you.

Stay on WordPress if your site does a lot beyond the newsletter (a store, memberships, courses, custom pages) and the email part already works, or if full control of your own hosting matters more to you than convenience.

2. Honest caveats

  • “Subscribers” can mean different things. Jetpack, MailPoet, and other plugins each store subscribers their own way, and some lists mix confirmed email subscribers with WordPress user accounts or commenters. Only move people who actually signed up for email.
  • Automations don’t port. Welcome emails, post notifications, and sequences need to be rebuilt in beehiiv.
  • Your blog SEO lives on WordPress. Moving posts to a new site can affect search traffic if you don’t plan redirects. Many people keep the blog where it is.
  • Paid members need care. If you charge through a WordPress membership plugin, billing usually can’t just be copied over. See section 8.
  • Costs differ. Compare what you pay now (plugin licenses, sending service, hosting) with beehiiv’s current plans for your list size. Check today’s pricing pages, not old blog posts.

3. Keep the blog, or move everything

You don’t have to choose all or nothing. Common patterns:

  • Blog stays, newsletter moves. WordPress keeps hosting your site and posts; beehiiv handles signups and sending. Lowest SEO risk, and the most common choice.
  • Newsletter-first. beehiiv becomes your main publication, and WordPress is reduced to a few static pages or retired over time.
  • Full move. Posts, subscribers, and the domain all go to beehiiv, with redirects from old WordPress URLs. Cleanest, most work, highest SEO risk.

If you aren’t sure, start with the first pattern. You can move more later.

4. Export subscribers from WordPress

Typical paths (verify in your plugin’s current docs and admin screens):

  • Jetpack subscriptions: look for the subscriber list and export option in your Jetpack or WordPress.com dashboard.
  • MailPoet: use its subscriber export to download a CSV, filtered by list if you have several.
  • Other plugins or a connected provider: export from wherever the list actually lives. If WordPress forms feed Mailchimp or MailerLite, export from that provider instead (see Mailchimp → beehiiv or MailerLite → beehiiv).

Then clean the file:

  • Keep only confirmed, subscribed addresses. Leave out unsubscribed, bounced, and unconfirmed contacts.
  • Don’t pull in WordPress user accounts or commenters who never signed up for email.
  • Note the signup date and any list or tag so you can keep that context.
  • Store the raw export offline before you change anything.

5. Import into beehiiv

  1. Create the publication first Branding, sender name, reply-to address, and a short welcome email that mentions your site so readers recognize you.
  2. Import the cleaned CSV Follow beehiiv’s current import steps and any review it requires for larger lists.
  3. Tag the source Add a tag like “from-wordpress” (and the old list name as a custom field) so you can segment later.
  4. Spot-check counts Compare imported totals with your cleaned export before you send anything.

6. Swap signup forms

  • Replace the plugin’s signup forms and widgets with a beehiiv embed form or a link to your beehiiv signup page. Check beehiiv’s docs for current embed options on your plan.
  • Update every place people subscribe: sidebar, footer, end-of-post boxes, pop-ups, and any landing pages.
  • Turn off the old plugin’s post notifications so readers don’t get two emails for one post.
  • Test a signup yourself, from the form to the welcome email.

7. Posts, archive, and SEO

  • If the blog stays, leave posts where they are and link to them from your newsletter issues.
  • If you move posts, check beehiiv’s current import options for existing content, or republish your best evergreen pieces by hand.
  • Redirect old URLs to their new homes if you retire WordPress pages, so search engines and old links don’t hit dead ends.
  • Keep a pointer on the old site, such as a short “the newsletter has moved” note with the new signup link.

8. Paid members and plugins

If you sell subscriptions through a WordPress membership plugin, slow down here.

  • Find out where payments actually run (often Stripe or PayPal through the plugin) and whether card details can move. In many setups they can’t, so members will need to subscribe again on beehiiv.
  • Give members a clear order: subscribe on beehiiv first, then cancel the old plan (or you end it on a set date), so nobody is charged twice.
  • Consider comped or free access on beehiiv for members whose current period overlaps the switch. Check beehiiv’s docs for what your plan supports.
  • New to paid on beehiiv? See start a paid newsletter on beehiiv.

9. Domain and sending setup

  • If you send from your own domain, add the SPF/DKIM records beehiiv asks for, plus a DMARC record if you don’t already have one. Keep a single SPF record per domain, and remove records for the old sending service only after the switch.
  • Use the sender name readers already know from your site.
  • For larger lists, send first to your most engaged readers and follow beehiiv’s warm-up guidance.

10. Cutover

  1. Send a heads-up from the old system Tell readers the newsletter is moving, what changes, and what sender name to expect.
  2. Final export Export again right before the switch to catch recent signups and unsubscribes.
  3. Switch forms and pause the plugin’s emails Do both on the same day so no new signups land in the old list.
  4. Send your first beehiiv issue Watch opens, bounces, and replies for the first few sends.
  5. Retire the old plugin last Deactivate it only after you’ve confirmed nothing else on the site depends on it and your exports are safe.

Final checklist

  • Decision made: blog stays, newsletter-first, or full move
  • Subscriber list exported, cleaned to confirmed subscribers, and backed up offline
  • Imported into beehiiv and tagged “from-wordpress”
  • Welcome email and any automations rebuilt
  • All signup forms swapped and tested
  • Old post notifications turned off (no double emails)
  • Paid members given a clear switch path, if you have any
  • Sending domain authenticated (if using your own domain)
  • Redirects in place for any retired URLs

If the newsletter has outgrown its plugin and you want sending, growth, and monetization in one place, beehiiv is the path this guide assumes. If WordPress email already works and your site does much more than send a newsletter, staying put may be the better call.
